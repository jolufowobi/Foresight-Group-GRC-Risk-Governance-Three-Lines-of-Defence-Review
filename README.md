# Foresight Group | GRC Risk Governance & Three Lines of Defence Review

## 1. Executive Summary

This case study examines the risk governance arrangements of Foresight Group Holdings Limited, an investment management business, with a focus on the Three Lines of Defence model and its application to cybersecurity and enterprise risk management.

The review considers how responsibilities are distributed across operational business functions, Risk and Compliance, and independent assurance. It also examines the role of the Board and Executive Committee in overseeing and managing the organisation's risk framework.

Particular attention is given to risk ownership, the Risk and Control Self-Assessment (RCSA) process, risk appetite, risk reporting, escalation and third-party cybersecurity risk.

The analysis also considers how these governance arrangements support the identification, assessment, monitoring and oversight of information security risks.

## 2. Organisational Context

Foresight Group Holdings Limited is an investment management business with activities spanning:

- Private markets.
- Renewable energy and sustainable infrastructure.
- Land and water investments.
- Technology and growing businesses.
- Listed investment trusts.

The nature of these activities exposes an investment management organisation to several categories of risk, including:

- Cybersecurity and information security risk.
- Financial and market risk.
- Regulatory and compliance risk.
- Operational risk.
- Third-party and supplier risk.
- Data confidentiality and integrity risk.

The organisation's business activities provide important context for understanding its risk governance arrangements. Risks arising from investment operations, information systems, external service providers and regulatory obligations require defined ownership, oversight and appropriate assurance.

## 3. The Three Lines of Defence Model

The Three Lines of Defence model establishes distinct responsibilities for managing risks, providing oversight and delivering independent assurance.

The model helps an organisation maintain accountability while ensuring that risk management is not treated as the responsibility of a single department.

### 3.1 First Line: Business and Operational Functions

**Primary responsibility: Risk ownership and day-to-day risk management.**

The first line consists of the business departments and operational functions responsible for carrying out the organisation's daily activities.

The case-study material identifies responsibilities including:

- Maintaining an adequate control environment.
- Following established risk policies and procedures.
- Managing risks arising from business and investment activities.
- Contributing to risk identification and scoring.
- Reporting and escalating incidents and losses.
- Providing information to support risk reporting.

The first line is closest to the activities that create operational risks and is responsible for managing those risks through its day-to-day processes.

**Cybersecurity application**

Consider a business department that handles confidential investment information.

The department is responsible for ensuring that:

- Access is restricted to authorised employees.
- Employees follow information security policies.
- Appropriate controls operate within its processes.
- Suspicious access and potential data exposure are reported.
- Identified control weaknesses are addressed.

The department does not transfer responsibility for these risks to the GRC or Risk function simply because that function provides oversight.

### 3.2 Second Line: Risk and Compliance

**Primary responsibility: Risk oversight, guidance, monitoring and challenge.**

The second line provides specialist risk and compliance oversight to support the organisation's risk management framework.

The case-study material identifies responsibilities including:

- Implementing and monitoring risk policies.
- Identifying, scoring and prioritising risks with input from the first line.
- Maintaining risk reporting.
- Advising on regulatory changes.
- Analysing losses and incidents.
- Monitoring investment limits.
- Supporting risk escalation.

The second line helps establish consistency in how risks are assessed and monitored across the organisation.

It also provides challenge where risk assessments, control arrangements or proposed remediation may not adequately address the exposure.

**Cybersecurity application**

Where a business department manages access to confidential investment information, the Risk or GRC function may:

- Review the department's risk assessment.
- Challenge whether identified risks have been appropriately rated.
- Monitor whether access reviews are taking place.
- Review reported control weaknesses.
- Monitor remediation progress.
- Escalate significant exposure through established governance channels.

The second line provides oversight and challenge, but does not automatically become the owner of the underlying business risk.

### 3.3 Third Line: Independent Assurance

**Primary responsibility: Independent assessment of governance, risk management and controls.**

The third line provides independent assurance over the adequacy and effectiveness of the organisation's risk management arrangements.

The Foresight case-study material identifies independent assurance responsibilities that include:

- Verifying the adequacy of the Group Risk Framework.
- Independently challenging the adequacy and effectiveness of actions taken by the Risk Committee.

In practice, this assurance role may be performed by Internal Audit or an appropriately independent assurance provider, depending on the organisation's arrangements.

**Cybersecurity application**

An independent assurance review could examine whether access management controls are appropriately designed and operating effectively.

This may involve:

- Reviewing access management policies.
- Examining access review records.
- Testing selected user permissions.
- Reviewing evidence of control operation.
- Assessing whether identified weaknesses have been addressed.
- Reporting assurance findings and recommendations.

The third line does not take over responsibility for managing risks or operating controls. Its role is to provide independent assurance.

## 4. Comparison of the Three Lines

| Area | First Line | Second Line | Third Line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary role | Risk ownership and management | Oversight and challenge | Independent assurance |
| Main responsibility | Operates business processes and controls | Provides risk frameworks, guidance, monitoring and reporting | Independently evaluates governance, risk management and controls |
| Risk ownership | Owns operational risks | Supports and challenges risk management | Does not own operational risks |
| Control responsibility | Operates controls | Monitors and challenges control arrangements | Independently assesses control effectiveness |
| Reporting | Reports operational risks, incidents and losses | Consolidates risk information and supports escalation | Reports independent assurance conclusions |
| Key question | Are we managing our risks? | Are risks being managed appropriately? | Are the governance and control arrangements effective? |

**A useful distinction:**

- First Line: Does the work and manages the associated risks.
- Second Line: Oversees and challenges how risks are managed.
- Third Line: Independently evaluates whether the arrangements are effective.

The Three Lines model describes different responsibilities. It does not necessarily require three entirely separate departments in every organisation.

## 5. Risk Governance and Accountability

The case-study material identifies the Board and Executive Committee as important components of Foresight's risk governance structure.

### 5.1 Board of Directors

The Board retains overall oversight and accountability for the Group Risk Framework.

Its role includes oversight of the organisation's approach to risk management and ensuring that appropriate governance arrangements are in place.

### 5.2 Executive Committee

The Board delegates responsibility for risk management to the Executive Committee.

This delegation establishes management-level accountability for coordinating and managing risk across the organisation.

Delegation does not remove the Board's overall oversight responsibility.

### 5.3 Risk Function

The Risk function supports the organisation's risk management arrangements through activities such as:

- Risk identification and assessment.
- Risk policy implementation and monitoring.
- Risk reporting.
- Risk analysis and prioritisation.
- Monitoring of risk exposure.
- Risk escalation.
- Providing oversight and challenge to business functions.

The Risk function supports management decisions by providing visibility into the organisation's risk exposure and the status of relevant risk management activities.

## 6. Risk Management Framework

The case-study material highlights several components of Foresight's risk management framework.

### 6.1 Risk and Control Self-Assessment (RCSA)

Risk and Control Self-Assessment is a process through which business areas identify and assess risks arising from their activities and consider the controls used to manage those risks.

The Foresight material specifically indicates that first-line resources identify and assess inherent risks alongside existing controls and their proven or expected mitigating effects.

The second line provides oversight and challenge to support consistency and appropriate risk assessment.

**Practical application**

Consider a business department that handles confidential investment information.

| RCSA Component | Example |
|---|---|
| Business activity | Handling confidential investment information |
| Inherent risk | Unauthorised disclosure of confidential information |
| Existing control | Email Data Loss Prevention (DLP) |
| Control assessment | Evaluate whether DLP is appropriately configured and operating as intended |
| Residual risk | Remaining exposure after considering the control |
| Oversight | Risk function reviews and challenges the assessment |

An important distinction is that the first line participates in assessing and managing its risks. The second line provides oversight rather than taking over that responsibility.

### 6.2 Risk Taxonomy

A risk taxonomy is an organised classification of risks.

It provides a consistent structure for categorising risks across the organisation and supports risk identification, assessment and reporting.

For an investment management organisation, risk categories may include operational, financial, regulatory, cybersecurity and third-party risks.

### 6.3 Risk Register

A risk register provides a structured record of identified risks and their management status.

Typical information includes:

- Risk description.
- Risk category.
- Risk owner.
- Inherent risk rating.
- Existing controls.
- Control assessment.
- Residual risk rating.
- Treatment actions.
- Action owners.
- Target dates.
- Risk status.

The risk register supports accountability, monitoring and escalation.

### 6.4 Risk Reporting and Escalation

Risk reporting provides management and governance bodies with information about the organisation's risk exposure.

Risk escalation ensures that significant risks, incidents and breaches are communicated to the appropriate level of management.

The Risk function supports the aggregation and reporting of risk information, while business functions remain responsible for identifying and reporting risks arising from their operations.

## 7. Risk Appetite and Governance

Foresight's Risk Appetite Statement sets out the types and levels of risk the Group is willing to accept or avoid while pursuing its business objectives.

The case-study material indicates that:

- Risk appetite provides direction for risk-taking and risk management decisions.
- The Risk Appetite Statement is reviewed annually.
- Information security risk is included among the areas covered by the organisation's risk appetite.

Risk appetite helps management determine whether an exposure is within acceptable boundaries or requires further action.

**Cybersecurity application**

An investment management organisation may accept certain levels of market risk as part of its investment strategy.

However, it may have a very low tolerance for unauthorised disclosure of confidential investor information or compromise of critical business systems.

Where cybersecurity exposure exceeds the organisation's approved risk appetite, management may need to strengthen controls, introduce additional treatment measures or escalate the matter for a risk decision.

## 8. Cybersecurity Risk Considerations

The case-study material highlights several areas where cybersecurity risk management is relevant to Foresight's operations.

### 8.1 Information Security and Data Protection

The protection of sensitive organisational and investment information requires appropriate safeguards.

The material highlights advanced encryption and data protection mechanisms as areas of cybersecurity focus.

These measures support the protection of information against unauthorised access and disclosure.

### 8.2 Penetration Testing

The case study identifies the use of authorised security professionals to simulate attacks as a way of testing cyber defences.

This describes penetration testing.

Penetration testing helps identify exploitable weaknesses by assessing how systems and applications respond to controlled attack scenarios.

The results can inform remediation priorities and provide input into the organisation's wider cybersecurity risk management activities.

### 8.3 Third-Party Cybersecurity Risk

External service providers can introduce cybersecurity exposure where they have access to organisational systems, information or infrastructure.

A supplier with inadequate security arrangements may become an entry point for attackers or contribute to a data breach.

The case-study material identifies detailed IT security assessments as part of third-party due diligence.

These assessments help evaluate whether a supplier's security arrangements are appropriate for the services it provides and the level of access it requires.

**Third-party due diligence considerations**

Depending on the nature of the service and the risk involved, an assessment may consider:

- Information security policies.
- Access management controls.
- Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA).
- Encryption arrangements.
- Incident response procedures.
- Breach notification obligations.
- Independent assurance reports.
- Data location and handling.
- Data retention and deletion practices.

Third-party risk should also be monitored throughout the supplier relationship, rather than assessed only before the contract is signed.

### 8.4 Emerging Technology Risk

The case-study material identifies the use of simple AI tools to improve phishing email quality as an emerging cybersecurity concern.

AI-enabled phishing can make fraudulent messages more convincing and harder for employees to recognise.

This increases the importance of employee awareness, email security controls, reporting procedures and ongoing threat monitoring.

## 9. Practical Application: Third-Party Cybersecurity Risk Across the Three Lines

Consider a scenario in which an external technology provider has inadequate access controls and provides services to Foresight.

The issue creates potential exposure because the provider may have access to company systems or confidential information.

The Three Lines model helps clarify how the organisation should manage the issue.

| Line | Responsibility | Practical Activities |
|---|---|---|
| First Line | Owns and manages the supplier-related business risk | Business team manages the supplier relationship, identifies the issue, assesses operational impact and ensures appropriate controls and remediation are addressed. |
| Second Line | Provides oversight and challenge | Risk function assesses the exposure, challenges the risk rating, monitors remediation and reports significant concerns. |
| Third Line | Provides independent assurance | Internal Audit or an appropriately independent assurance provider reviews whether third-party risk management arrangements are adequately designed and operating effectively. |

### GRC Analyst Application

A GRC Analyst working within the second line may support this process by:

- Coordinating risk assessments.
- Reviewing supplier security assessment results.
- Maintaining risk register information.
- Monitoring remediation actions.
- Tracking overdue control improvements.
- Preparing risk reports.
- Escalating significant exposures.

However, coordinating the assessment or maintaining the risk register does not automatically make the GRC Analyst the owner of the underlying business risk.

Risk ownership should remain with the appropriate business stakeholder.

## 10. Key GRC Observations

The review highlights several important governance and risk management principles.

**1. Risk ownership must remain clear.**

Business and operational functions are responsible for managing risks arising from their activities. Risk and Compliance provides oversight but does not automatically assume ownership.

**2. Oversight requires challenge.**

The second line should do more than collect risk information. It provides guidance, monitors exposure and challenges assessments or treatment decisions where necessary.

**3. Independent assurance is different from management oversight.**

The third line provides an independent perspective on whether governance, risk management and controls are adequate and effective.

**4. Risk appetite informs management decisions.**

Risk appetite helps determine whether an organisation's exposure is acceptable and whether additional treatment or escalation is required.

**5. RCSA connects business activities to risk and controls.**

The first line contributes to identifying and assessing inherent risks and considering the mitigating effects of existing controls. The second line provides oversight and challenge.

**6. Third-party risk requires ongoing attention.**

Supplier due diligence is important because external providers may introduce cybersecurity exposure through their access to organisational systems and information.

**7. Control documentation does not establish effectiveness.**

The existence of a policy or control description is not sufficient assurance. Appropriate evidence and independent assessment may be required to determine whether controls operate as intended.
